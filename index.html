Reference desk

Rulebook.

A compact guide to the rules most useful when constructing ordinary organic names. Section labels refer to the IUPAC Blue Book 2013.

P-59 / NAME CONSTRUCTION

Construct the name in a deliberate order

  1. Determine the type of nomenclature and operations required for the compound.
  2. Determine the compound class and the principal characteristic group to be cited as the suffix, if applicable.
  3. Select the senior parent hydride or functional parent structure.
  4. Name the parent and principal characteristic group.
  5. Determine suffixes, prefixes and multiplicative prefixes.
  6. Name substituents and non-principal characteristic groups as prefixes.
  7. Assemble components using locants, ordering and name-writing conventions.
  8. Add all required stereochemical descriptors.

Only one kind of characteristic group is normally cited as the principal suffix in substitutive nomenclature. Other groups are expressed as prefixes. The full recommendations include exceptions and specialised naming types.

P-44 / P-2 / P-5

Select the senior parent, not merely the longest chain

Parent selection is hierarchical. The structure containing the principal characteristic group is central to the decision. The Blue Book then applies criteria for parent seniority, which differ for acyclic chains and ring systems.

  • For acyclic parent chains, compare the applicable criteria for skeletal atoms and heteroatoms, then further structural features when needed.
  • For rings and ring systems, special criteria apply, including ring-system type, heteroatoms, ring count and skeletal atom count.
  • Only after choosing the parent should the complete compound be numbered under the applicable numbering rules.
HSC shortcut: for many simple branched hydrocarbons, choosing the longest suitable chain works. Treat it as a shortcut, not a universal IUPAC rule.
P-14.3–P-14.4 / P-59.1.10

Locants and numbering

Locants identify positions in a parent structure. They are normally placed immediately before the name part they describe. Compare locant sets at the first point of difference, following the applicable priority order rather than simply adding the numbers.

  1. Respect any fixed numbering defined for the parent structure.
  2. Give heteroatoms their required locants in heteroatom-containing parents.
  3. Consider indicated hydrogen where applicable.
  4. Give the principal suffix group the lowest appropriate locant.
  5. Consider added indicated hydrogen and hydro/dehydro or ene/yne features as applicable.
  6. Then allocate locants to substituent prefixes, using the detailed rules for ties.

For many ordinary HSC structures, remember: principal functional group before unsaturation, and unsaturation before ordinary substituent prefixes. More complex parents have additional criteria.

P-14.2 / P-14.5 / P-35

Prefixes and alphabetical order

Prefixes identify substituents and characteristic groups that are not represented by the principal suffix. Simple prefixes are arranged in alphanumerical order under the Blue Book's rules.

  • Common prefixes: methyl-, ethyl-, bromo-, chloro-, fluoro-, iodo-, hydroxy-, amino-, oxo-, nitro- and alkoxy-.
  • Basic multiplicative prefixes such as di-, tri- and tetra- indicate repetition. They are generally not used to determine the alphabetical position of a simple substituent.
  • Bis-, tris- and tetrakis- are used for more complex repeated entities, often with enclosing marks.
  • Locants are attached to the feature they identify. Separate compound substituents may require parentheses or brackets.

P-33 / P-15.1.4

Parent endings, unsaturation and suffixes

For ordinary acyclic hydrocarbons, the parent hydride ending changes to reflect unsaturation: -ane for saturated chains, -ene for double bonds and -yne for triple bonds. Locants identify the bond position.

When a suffix beginning with a vowel is attached, the terminal 'e' of the parent hydride may be elided according to the Blue Book's rules. For example, ethane + ol gives ethanol, while propan-2-ol uses the form 'propan'.

P-16 / NAME WRITING

Spelling, punctuation and spaces

  • Commas separate numerical locants: 2,3-dimethylbutane.
  • Hyphens separate locants from name components: butan-2-ol.
  • Spaces separate distinct words in names where the nomenclature requires them, such as 'ethanoic acid' and 'methyl ethanoate'.
  • Parentheses and brackets group complex substituents and descriptors.
  • Do not invent spaces inside a continuous substitutive name. Follow the specific name-writing rule for the nomenclature type.
The formal Blue Book contains detailed rules for spaces, elision, enclosing marks, italics and primes. This panel is a quick reminder, not the complete P-16 text.
P-9 / STEREOCHEMISTRY

Configuration and conformation

Stereodescriptors are added when the structure specifies stereogenic features. Common descriptors include R/S for tetrahedral stereogenic centres and E/Z for double-bond configuration, assigned using the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog sequence rules.

(2R)-...   ·   (3E)-...

Do not guess a stereodescriptor from a condensed formula that does not provide sufficient stereochemical information.

What this website does not replace

This site is an educational companion. The Blue Book covers a much wider field than ordinary introductory organic nomenclature, including polycyclic and fused ring systems, heterocycles, replacement nomenclature, salts, radicals, isotopically modified compounds, natural products and detailed stereochemistry.

When a problem involves one of these cases, use the relevant Blue Book chapter rather than applying a simplified chain-naming shortcut.

Quick recognition

Functional-group patterns

Carbonyl family

C=O compounds

Aldehydes have a terminal –CHO group. Ketones have a carbonyl carbon bonded to two carbon-containing groups. Carboxylic acids have –COOH.

Oxygen family

O-containing groups

Alcohols contain –OH; ethers contain R–O–R′; esters contain –COOR. Their naming methods are not interchangeable.

Nitrogen family

N-containing groups

Amines contain nitrogen with single bonds; amides contain a carbonyl attached to nitrogen; nitriles contain –C≡N.
